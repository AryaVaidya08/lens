## Basics
Lorazepam is a benzodiazepine indicated for the management of anxiety disorders
and for the short-term relief of symptoms of anxiety. It is a Schedule IV
controlled substance. Its intermediate half-life and absence of active metabolites
make it a common choice in patients with hepatic impairment.
- Anxiety disorders and short-term relief of anxiety symptoms
- Schedule IV controlled substance
- No active metabolites, so it is often preferred in hepatic impairment

## Dosing
The usual oral dose for anxiety is 2 to 3 mg per day given in two or three divided
doses, with a range of 1 to 10 mg per day individualized to the patient. Start at
1 to 2 mg per day in elderly or debilitated patients. Treatment is intended to be
short term, and the need for it should be reassessed frequently.
- Usual anxiety dose: 2 to 3 mg per day in divided doses
- Elderly or debilitated patients: start at 1 to 2 mg per day
- Intended for short-term use with frequent reassessment

## Side effects
The most frequent adverse effects are sedation, dizziness, weakness, and
unsteadiness, all of which are dose related and more pronounced in elderly
patients. Anterograde amnesia and paradoxical disinhibition occur less commonly.
Falls are the practical concern in older adults.
- Common: sedation, dizziness, weakness, unsteadiness
- Less common: anterograde amnesia and paradoxical disinhibition
- Fall risk is the practical concern in elderly patients

## Warnings
Lorazepam carries boxed warnings for concomitant use with opioids, for abuse,
misuse, and addiction, and for dependence with withdrawal reactions. Combining it
with opioids can cause profound sedation, respiratory depression, coma, and death.
Do not stop abruptly after sustained use; taper gradually, because abrupt
discontinuation can precipitate seizures and other acute withdrawal reactions.
- Boxed warning: profound sedation and respiratory depression with opioids
- Boxed warning: abuse, misuse, addiction, dependence, and withdrawal
- Taper gradually; abrupt discontinuation can precipitate seizures

## Interactions
Alcohol and other central nervous system depressants compound sedation and
respiratory depression. Valproate and probenecid reduce lorazepam clearance and
raise plasma levels, so reduce the lorazepam dose when either is started.
Clozapine combined with lorazepam has been associated with marked sedation.
- Alcohol and CNS depressants compound sedation
- Valproate and probenecid raise lorazepam levels; reduce the dose
- Clozapine combination has been associated with marked sedation

## Trial data
Controlled trials in generalized anxiety showed symptom reduction over four to six
weeks, with benefit appearing within the first days of treatment, which is faster
than the onset of SSRIs. Longer-term data are limited, and studies consistently
show tolerance to sedative effects along with dependence risk that grows with
duration of use, which is why guidelines favor time-limited courses.
- Anxiety symptom reduction within days, faster onset than SSRIs
- Efficacy data concentrated in 4 to 6 week courses
- Dependence risk grows with duration, supporting time-limited use
