## Basics
Adderall is a combination of mixed amphetamine salts, indicated for attention
deficit hyperactivity disorder and narcolepsy. It is a Schedule II controlled
substance. Immediate-release tablets and extended-release capsules are both
available, and they are not interchangeable milligram for milligram.
- Indicated for ADHD and narcolepsy
- Schedule II: prescribing and refills are restricted
- Immediate-release and extended-release forms are not interchangeable

## Dosing
Immediate-release tablets are usually started at 5 mg once or twice daily and
titrated weekly in 5 mg increments to clinical response. The extended-release
capsule is taken once each morning and should not be dosed in the afternoon
because of insomnia risk. Doses above 40 mg per day are rarely more effective.
- Immediate release: start 5 mg once or twice daily, titrate weekly
- Extended release: 20 mg once each morning, never in the afternoon
- Reassess the need for treatment periodically rather than continuing by default

## Side effects
The most common adverse reactions are decreased appetite, insomnia, dry mouth,
headache, weight loss, irritability, and increased heart rate. Most are dose
related and improve with dose reduction or by moving the dose earlier in the day.
- Common: decreased appetite, insomnia, dry mouth, headache, weight loss
- Cardiovascular: increased heart rate and blood pressure
- Most effects are dose related and improve with dose reduction

## Warnings
Adderall carries a boxed warning for abuse, misuse, and addiction, including the
risk of overdose and death. Assess cardiovascular risk before starting, since
sudden death has been reported in patients with structural cardiac abnormalities.
Monitor for new or worsening psychosis, mania, and peripheral vasculopathy.
- Boxed warning: abuse, misuse, and addiction
- Screen for structural cardiac disease before starting
- Monitor blood pressure, heart rate, weight, and growth in children

## Interactions
Avoid use within 14 days of a monoamine oxidase inhibitor because of the risk of
hypertensive crisis. Serotonergic agents raise the risk of serotonin syndrome.
Urinary acidifying agents lower amphetamine exposure, and alkalinizing agents
raise it, so gastrointestinal and urinary pH changes are clinically relevant.
- Contraindicated within 14 days of an MAO inhibitor
- Serotonergic drugs increase serotonin syndrome risk
- Urinary pH changes meaningfully shift amphetamine levels

## Trial data
Registration trials in children and adolescents showed improvement on the ADHD
Rating Scale within the first week of treatment, with effect sizes that separated
from placebo at every dose studied. Adult extended-release trials showed a similar
separation, with reductions in total ADHD symptom scores sustained through four
weeks. Discontinuation for adverse events was driven mainly by insomnia,
decreased appetite, and increased heart rate rather than by lack of efficacy.
- Separation from placebo on ADHD Rating Scale within the first week
- Adult extended-release benefit sustained through four weeks
- Discontinuations driven by insomnia and appetite loss, not loss of effect
