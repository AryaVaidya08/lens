## Inspiration

Healthcare professionals are constantly surrounded by physical pharmaceuticals, but the drugs themselves don't stay constant. In just the last five years, the FDA has approved over 230 new drugs, and that pace makes it functionally impossible for any HCP to stay current on everything they've learned before and everything currently passing through their hands. The gap isn't in what they already know — a physician generally has the established, high-volume drugs in their specialty memorized cold. It's the constant stream of new products where that depth runs thin, and the question that matters in the moment isn't academic: is this specific sample actually safe to give to the patient about to walk in.

Rather than automated text messages that could be ignored, or physically searching up information that could be misleading or out of date, Lens meets the physician at the one moment that's already guaranteed to happen — picking the sample up. Impiricus's GTHacks challenge asked teams to invent a third way to engage HCPs beyond push (a message the physician has to notice and choose not to ignore) and pull (a search bar, waiting for the physician to already know they need to look something up). Lens is that third way: it turns the physical act of holding a drug sample into the interface itself, so the app can tell the difference between a drug the physician already knows cold and one where grounded, up-to-date safety information is exactly what they need right now.

## What it does

Lens turns a physical drug sample into a personalized, spatial clinical touchpoint. A clinician signs into the iOS app, optionally selects a patient they're seeing, and points their phone at the sample. On-device, a Create ML–trained object detector finds the bottle in frame, Vision's barcode reader is tried against it first, and on-device OCR reads the label if no barcode resolves — all before anything leaves the phone. Once the drug is identified, ARKit anchors a heads-up display directly onto the physical object in space.

What that HUD actually shows is the core of the product, and it isn't static. Lens tracks how many times this specific clinician has scanned this specific drug before, and uses that count to decide what to lead with: the first scan surfaces the basics (what the drug is, what it's for); the second moves to dosing and precautions; the third and beyond jumps straight to clinical pharmacology, interactions, and mechanism of action — the same drug, three visibly different conversations, all pulled from the same reference material. Layered on top of that, the clinician's own specialty reshapes which sections get prioritized, so a cardiologist and a pediatrician scanning the same bottle at the same familiarity level still see different information surfaced first.

If a patient is selected, Lens quietly checks that patient's recorded allergies and current medications against the scanned drug's warnings and contraindications, flagging anything worth a second look directly on the HUD — not a diagnosis, just a nudge to look closer. The clinician can then ask a question out loud — "what's the boxed warning?" — and Lens retrieves the relevant passage from a knowledge base of over 1,800 openFDA drug dossiers, generates a grounded, spoken-length-appropriate answer, and reads it back, so the clinician never has to look away from the sample or type anything.

Every scan and every question gets logged, which is what makes the next scan different from this one. A companion React web dashboard gives the same clinician a desktop view of that history: past conversations, patients, a medication-review tool that OCRs a bottle label and checks it against a patient's recorded medication list, and engagement history across every drug they've encountered.

## How we built it

The detection stage runs entirely on-device: a Create ML object detector locates the bottle, Vision handles barcode decoding, and Vision's OCR is the fallback when there's no barcode to read — all via SwiftUI, ARKit, and the Speech/AVSpeechSynthesizer stack for the voice interaction. The backend is a FastAPI service backed by real MongoDB, with a small purpose-built scoring module that converts a clinician's prior touch count on a drug into a familiarity tier, and a specialty-to-dossier-section mapping layered on top of it — that's the entire personalization engine, and it's cheap enough to run on every request. Retrieval runs against an in-memory index built at startup from our 1,800+ document openFDA corpus, embedded with `sentence-transformers`, ranked by cosine similarity with a bonus for chunks whose section name matches the question — which is what lets "what's the boxed warning" reliably land on the boxed warning instead of whatever paragraph happens to say "warning" the most. Retrieved context goes to xAI's Grok for generation, with a system prompt that scales answer length to familiarity tier and forbids inventing anything not present in the retrieved text, since the answer gets spoken aloud and has to be trustworthy without a citation on screen. Auth is real — PBKDF2-hashed passwords, hashed session tokens, rate-limited login — because a clinician's engagement history and patient records had to actually be theirs, not something a demo could fake with a client-supplied ID. The web dashboard is React, hitting the same API the iOS app does.

## Challenges we ran into

The hardest problem wasn't any single system — it was keeping the whole pipeline fast enough that pointing a camera at a bottle still *feels* like pointing a camera at a bottle, not like submitting a search query and waiting. Detection, drug resolution, familiarity scoring, patient chart checking, and HUD rendering all have to resolve before the AR overlay appears, and a personalization feature that adds a visible half-second stall in front of a live audience defeats its own purpose. The second-hardest problem was almost the opposite: making the personalization *change* obvious enough, fast enough, that someone watching a live demo could tell scan two apart from scan one without having it explained to them — which is why tier changes the HUD's headline and content, not just an invisible ranking weight underneath.

## Accomplishments that we're proud of

We're proud that Lens isn't a computer vision demo bolted onto a chatbot — it's on-device object detection, ARKit spatial anchoring, a from-scratch personalization engine, patient-aware context injection, retrieval-augmented voice generation, and a real authenticated data layer, all agreeing with each other closely enough that a clinician can scan the same bottle twice and watch the experience visibly change the second time. Getting that many independently complex systems to stay in sync, in real time, on a physical device, was the actual project.

## What we learned

The biggest lesson was that the personalization loop is what makes Lens a product instead of a search tool — and that loop only had to track two small pieces of state, a touch count and a specialty, to produce a meaningfully different experience on every repeat interaction. The harder-won lesson was that the value of that state is entirely dependent on every downstream system actually reading it consistently; the HUD, the retrieval ranking, and the voice answer all had to agree on what "returning" or "expert" meant, or the personalization would feel arbitrary instead of intentional.

## What's next for Lens

We'd move clinician verification to something NPI-backed instead of self-registered, connect patient context to a real EHR instead of manual entry, and swap the in-memory retrieval index for a hosted vector database sitting behind the same interface we already built — the architecture was designed as a set of swap points specifically so none of that requires touching the rest of the app. Longer term, we want engagement analytics that show a clinician (or a pharma partner) how familiarity with a drug actually evolves across a physician's whole patient panel over time, not just within a single scan.
